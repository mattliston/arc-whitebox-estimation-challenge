(5) COMPETITION TASK

There is one (1) task in this Competition: predict the per-neuron mean activation of randomly-initialized ReLU multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) under standard-normal inputs, subject to an analytical FLOP budget. For each evaluation instance, Sponsor provides one MLP and one FLOP budget; the Participant's program ("Solution") must emit a (layers, width) array of mean-activation predictions, which is scored by mean squared error (MSE) against a high-budget Monte-Carlo reference target.
5.1 Task

Each evaluation instance is one randomly-initialized ReLU MLP drawn from a generative distribution published in the official starter kit. The width and depth of the evaluation MLPs are fixed for each Phase and published for that Phase, and may differ between Phases. In Phase 1 the evaluation MLPs are 256 neurons wide and 32 hidden layers deep. In Phase 2 they are 1,024 neurons wide and 16 hidden layers deep. Inputs are sampled from a standard-normal distribution of dimension matching the MLP's input width. The Solution must return, for each evaluation MLP, a (layers, width) array whose entry (ℓ, i) is the Solution's estimate of the expected post-ReLU activation of neuron i in hidden layer ℓ under the input distribution. Submissions are scored by mean squared error against a high-budget Monte-Carlo reference target computed by Sponsor with substantially more compute than is available to Participants.

Participants submit code (an estimator.py exposing the estimator contract published in the official starter kit) which will be executed by the AIcrowd grader against a held-out suite of randomly-initialized MLPs. The starter kit, referenced from the Competition Site, contains the canonical specification of the estimator contract, the scoring pipeline, the submission packaging format, the flopscope accounting library, each Phase's per-MLP FLOP budget and, for Phase 1, the residual wall-time conversion rate, and example estimators. In the event of an inconsistency between these Rules and the starter kit, these Rules govern; any starter-kit detail that materially affects scoring or eligibility will also be reflected here or on the Competition Site.

5.4 Test sets and leaderboards

The Competition uses three kinds of test material:

1. Public verification examples. A small set of randomly-initialized MLPs and corresponding reference outputs is released openly via the starter kit so that Participants can debug their Solutions and check that their local setup matches the remote grader. These are not used for leaderboard scoring or ranking.

2. Per-Phase test suite. During each official Phase, the AIcrowd grader evaluates each submission on a private test suite of one hundred (100) randomly generated MLPs whose weights, seeds, and reference outputs are not disclosed to Participants. This suite is split into:

    a public subset of fifty (50) MLPs whose scores are shown on the live leaderboard during the Phase; and
    a private subset of fifty (50) MLPs whose scores are graded in parallel but withheld until the Phase closes.

Phase-end standings combine scores across both subsets (all 100 MLPs). The public/private split keeps the live leaderboard useful for Participant feedback while limiting over-tuning to the visible scores.

3. Private Re-evaluation test suites. Following the close of each Phase, Sponsor re-executes each eligible Participant's designated submissions for that Phase (see §6) against a separate, fresh, never-seen test suite of randomly-initialized MLPs generated for that Phase from private seeds that were not used in any Phase test suite or in any other Private Re-evaluation. Each such re-run produces that Phase's final (private) leaderboard, and that Phase's prize ranking is decided exclusively from it — not from any score visible on the public leaderboard during that Phase. Each Private Re-evaluation test suite is drawn from the same distribution as that Phase's test suite (same width and depth, same FLOP-budget calibration) but uses different random MLPs, so Solutions that overfit to specific MLPs or specific seeds will be penalized accordingly. Sponsor determines the number of MLPs in each Private Re-evaluation test suite and will evaluate as many MLPs as Sponsor considers necessary to establish that Phase's final ranking with confidence, including by drawing additional MLPs from the same distribution where required for that purpose.

If leading submissions remain statistically indistinguishable after the Private Re-evaluation, Sponsor may generate additional MLPs from the same distribution to reduce uncertainty, per the tie-breaking procedure in §6.
5.5 Scoring and budget

Primary scoring metric. Solutions are scored by mean squared error (MSE) against a high-budget Monte-Carlo reference target — lower is better. The reference target is estimated using a much larger computational budget than is available to Participants, for precision. The primary metric is the MSE of the Participant's final-layer predictions; the all-layer MSE (MSE across all hidden layers) is reported alongside as a secondary diagnostic. The exact form of the per-MLP score, including its aggregation across the test suite and normalization, is defined by the scoring pipeline distributed with the official starter kit; Sponsor may refine details of this implementation as described in §5.3.

Effective compute budget. Each MLP is evaluated under an effective FLOP budget, and the way effective cost is measured differs between Phases. In Phase 1, the effective cost of an MLP is the sum of:

    Analytical FLOPs counted by flopscope — hardware-independent, computed from tensor shapes and operation kinds; and
    The Participant's residual wall-clock time outside flopscope operations, converted to FLOPs at an unfavorable per-second rate published on the Competition Site and in the starter kit.

In Phase 2, no conversion of residual time applies: the effective cost of an MLP is the analytical FLOP count reported by flopscope alone, and residual time is instead subject to the cap described in §5.6. The effective cost is checked against the per-MLP FLOP budget, which is fixed for each Phase and published for that Phase in the starter kit. The per-MLP budget is approximately 2.72 × 10^11 FLOPs in Phase 1 and 2^41 FLOPs (approximately 2.199 × 10^12) in Phase 2. The per-MLP FLOP budget will not change during a Phase except as provided in §5.3. In Phase 1, this design lets Participants use non-flopscope code while ensuring that off-flopscope computation is charged against the budget; in Phase 2, computation outside flopscope is not permitted, as set out in §5.2.

Budget-adjusted leaderboard score. Submissions that use less than the full FLOP budget on a given MLP are awarded an improvement to their MSE, capped relative to full-budget usage so that cheap-but-inaccurate Solutions cannot dominate the ranking. For an evaluation MLP m, the per-MLP score is:

s_m = MSE_final,m × max( 0.1 , C_m / B_m )

where MSE_final,m is the final-layer mean squared error for that MLP, B_m is the per-MLP FLOP budget, and C_m is the effective cost of that MLP. The effective cost differs between Phases:

Phase 1: C_m = F_m + λ · R_m
Phase 2: C_m = F_m

where F_m is the analytical FLOP count reported by flopscope, R_m is the residual wall-clock time, and λ is the published conversion rate. In Phase 2, residual time is not priced into C_m; it is instead subject to the cap described in §5.6.
The overall leaderboard score is the average of s_m across the evaluation suite.
Fair accounting. The score benefit of a submission must derive from its estimation method. Sponsor may determine, in its sole discretion, at any time — including during or after grading, and during prize review — that a technique derives its benefit from the manner in which computation is accounted rather than from the estimation it performs, and may invalidate, re-score, or disqualify affected submissions accordingly. Sponsor's determination under this section is final.
To help Participants understand how Sponsor expects to exercise this discretion, and without limiting it in any way, Sponsor offers the following explanation of intent. This explanation and its examples are illustrative only; they are not exhaustive, do not define or limit the scope of Sponsor's discretion, and — except for the written-confirmation reliance described at the end of this section — create no rights or expectations in any Participant.
flopscope prices what a Solution declares: an analytical charge computed from the kinds and shapes of the operations presented to it, not from how any particular hardware executes them. The published cost model specification, referenced from the starter kit, is the statement of what each operation is intended to cost. The intent is that declarations be faithful — each charged element carrying one logical value of the method's computation, and each declared operation being the mathematical operation the method performs. On faithful declarations, every discount the published cost model grants is intended: symmetry-aware pricing that charges a provably symmetric result at its reduced count is there to be used, as is every other price the model deliberately sets. This section is concerned with the two ways a Solution can be built to be charged for less computation than its method performs: declarations that understate the computation, and charges that diverge from the published specification.
Techniques of the first kind include packing several independent values into a single machine element — for example, packing boolean values into wider integer types and operating on them with bitwise operations: the method logically operates on each boolean value, but declares one wider element carrying thirty-two of them. The declaration understates the computation, and the charge follows the declaration.
The second kind involves the cost model itself. Where the implementation's charge diverges from the published specification, that is a bug to which §5.3 applies: Sponsor may fix it and re-grade affected submissions under the corrected model, and a Solution has no expectation of retaining any advantage that a mispricing conferred. Deliberately constructing a Solution around such a divergence — for example, transforming inputs in ways that serve no purpose for the estimate and exist only to move the computation onto a cheaper-charged path — may additionally be treated under this section. A Participant who finds a divergence between the specification and the charge is encouraged to report it to arc-whestbench@aicrowd.com; helpful reports of this kind are among the contributions the discretionary community-contribution awards described in §6 recognize.
By contrast, genuinely reducing the computation a method requires is the point of the Competition: reducing precision where the method tolerates it, exploiting mathematical structure and symmetry, and choosing better algorithms are all encouraged.
In short: an advantage that comes from how computation was described or priced, rather than from how little computation the method needs, is not an estimation advance.
Where the cost model can be corrected or extended to price a technique accurately, Sponsor will prefer doing so over case-by-case determinations, and any such change will be announced as described in §5.3. A Participant who is unsure whether a technique is consistent with this section should write to arc-whestbench@aicrowd.com, describing the technique and identifying a relevant submission where applicable, before relying on it. A Participant may rely on Sponsor's written confirmation that a specific, fully described technique is permissible, for the submissions to which that confirmation is addressed.

Zero-prediction fallback. Submissions whose effective FLOP usage on a given MLP exceeds the budget, raise an exception, return non-finite values, return an unparseable result, exhaust memory, exceed the wall-clock cap or the residual-time cap described in §5.6, or otherwise fail to produce a usable prediction will have their prediction for that MLP replaced with a fallback value of zero at all layers and assigned the corresponding zero-prediction MSE for that MLP. The grader continues evaluating the remaining MLPs in the suite. There is no retry mechanism and no opportunity to re-submit after the deadline. Participants are strongly encouraged to test their Solution broadly across MLPs drawn from the published generative distribution before submission and to write defensive code that degrades gracefully rather than failing outright on unusual inputs. Sponsor reserves discretion to handle infrastructure-level failures (failures attributable to the grader rather than the submission) on a case-by-case basis.

Reproducibility under fixed seeds. Submissions may use randomness (Monte Carlo, sampling-based methods, randomized hybrids). For each MLP in the per-Phase and Private Re-evaluation test suites, the grader supplies a fixed random seed identical across all submissions on that MLP; prize-eligible submissions must produce reproducible predictions under this seed. Participants may use any seeds they wish during local development, but each Private Re-evaluation runs each Solution once per MLP under the grader's seed, and Participants are responsible for managing their own estimator variance within the published compute budget. The deterministic grader environment (CPU-only, pinned dependencies — see §5.6) is designed to keep stochastic-estimator results reproducible across re-runs.
5.6 Hardware and system configuration

All submissions are evaluated on the grader's standard evaluation instance, with the following specifications:

    Each evaluation is allocated sixteen (16) vCPUs in total. The Solution's own process is pinned to an exclusive allocation of two (2) vCPUs — one whole physical core — and the remaining fourteen (14) vCPUs are used by the flopscope backend that executes metered operations and by the evaluation harness. This allocation is the same in Phase 1 and Phase 2.

    64 GB RAM in total. In Phase 2, the Solution's own process is limited to eight (8) GB, with the remainder available to the flopscope backend and the evaluation harness; exhausting that limit on a given MLP is handled by the zero-prediction fallback in §5.5.

    Hard wall-clock cap per MLP — fixed for each Phase and published for that Phase. The cap is sixty (60) seconds in Phase 1 and one hundred and twenty (120) seconds in Phase 2. Solutions that exceed the cap on a given MLP trigger the zero-prediction fallback in §5.5. At the published FLOP budget, only stalled or runaway Solutions should reach this cap. Sponsor may increase, but will not reduce, the wall-clock cap during a Phase where necessary for evaluation throughput or reliability; any such increase will be announced. Because this cap itself does not enter the scoring metric — in Phase 1, residual wall-clock time is charged under §5.5 at the published conversion rate regardless of this cap — an increase to this cap is not a scoring-affecting change. In Phase 1, a Participant's residual wall-clock time outside flopscope operations is converted into FLOPs at a published conversion rate and charged against the per-MLP budget. No such conversion applies in Phase 2, in which all computation must be performed through flopscope. Separately, a Solution's one-time import and setup phase is subject to its own cap of five (5) seconds. Setup runs once, before any evaluation MLP is assigned, so exceeding this cap fails the entire submission rather than triggering the per-MLP zero-prediction fallback in §5.5.

    Network access disabled during evaluation — Solutions must bundle all weights, lookup tables, and precomputed artifacts inside the submission tarball.

    CPU-only execution — GPU frameworks are not available on the grader instance.

    Grader-provided Python environment — a Solution runs in a dedicated, minimal Python environment, with pinned package versions, containing the flopscope client, the WhestBench participant interface, and the Python standard library. NumPy is not installed in that environment in any Phase, and import numpy raises ModuleNotFoundError. Solutions may use import flopscope.numpy as fnp, but the flopscope client performs no computation locally: each operation is dispatched to a separate, trusted flopscope backend process, which performs the computation, meters its analytical FLOP cost, and returns the result. All array computation therefore takes place in the metered backend. Common scientific libraries — including SciPy, scikit-learn, JAX, PyTorch, Numba, and Cython — are likewise not installed.
    In Phase 1, Participants may bundle additional Python dependencies or precompiled artifacts with their submission tarball, and computation performed in such non-flopscope code is charged against the budget through the residual-wall-time conversion described in §5.5; this is not permitted in Phase 2, as set out in §5.2.

The scoring metric (§5.5) depends on effective cost and prediction values; the hard wall-clock cap does not itself enter the score. The wall-clock cap and other hardware-sensitive limits exist to keep the evaluation queue reliable; the primary compute constraint is the effective FLOP budget. Solutions tuned on noticeably faster hardware than the grader may exceed the wall-clock cap on the grader even when they pass locally — Participants are responsible for testing on hardware comparable to the grader specification.

Residual time. Wall-clock time within a Solution's predict() call that is not attributable to flopscope operations is that Solution's residual time. In Phase 1, residual time may carry non-flopscope computation, which is charged against the budget at the published conversion rate under §5.5. In Phase 2, residual time exists only so that a Solution's Python can loop, index, keep bookkeeping, and decide which flopscope operations to call next, and is capped at four hundred (400) milliseconds per MLP; an MLP whose residual time exceeds that cap receives the zero-prediction fallback described in §5.5, in the same way as a wall-clock timeout.
In Phase 2, residual time is for plumbing, not for computation: performing meaningful computational work in residual time, by any mechanism, is a breach of these Rules and is grounds for disqualification of the submission. Ordinary control flow, indexing, bookkeeping, and the assembly of arguments to flopscope operations are legitimate uses of residual time. That list is not exhaustive, and whether particular work performed in residual time constitutes meaningful computation is determined by Sponsor in its sole discretion.

Sponsor may implement automated checks that monitor the Solution's execution environment during evaluation and invalidate submissions that breach this section, and may use LLM-assisted review in enforcing these Rules. Such review may be carried out after a submission has been graded, and a submission found to be in breach may be invalidated retrospectively. Sponsor's determination under this section is final.

A Participant whose Solution has a legitimate need for more residual time than the cap allows should contact Sponsor at arc-whestbench@aicrowd.com, identifying the relevant submission, before relying on it. Any change to the cap will be announced to all Participants.
