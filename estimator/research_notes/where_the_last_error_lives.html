Where the remaining error comes from

Our final-layer error is 1.712e-8. Almost none of it is made at the last layer: each layer from 5 to 15 adds 1.6 to 3.8e-9 of new error through three per-unit inputs of one formula, and about 83% of every layer's error is passed on to the next.

The competition gives a random network with 16 layers of 1,024 ReLU units and asks for the average output of every unit when the input is Gaussian noise. The score of one network is the mean-squared error of the 1,024 final-layer averages, multiplied by the share of the compute budget used, with a minimum share of 10%. Each entry is run on 100 networks; the board shows the average over the 50 whose scores are public, and lower is better. Every number below says what it is, on which networks it was measured and against what.

02 · the estimator

What the estimator carries from layer to layer

The estimator never runs the network on sample inputs: it carries a description of the distribution of each layer's outputs and updates that description with formulas, one layer at a time. It carries five things.

  1. The mean of each of the 1,024 units.
  2. The covariance of every pair of units, a table of 1,024 × 1,024 numbers.
  3. Third-order structure (how lopsided each unit's distribution is), stored as a bank of columns: two new columns for every unit that is close to its switching point at each layer, pushed through the weights at every later layer, with the older columns compressed to 384, 320 and 384 directions at layers 7, 10 and 13.
  4. Fourth-order structure (how heavy the tails are) as one number per layer, used for all units alike.
  5. A learned correction: a small network with about 45,000 trained parameters that adjusts each unit's mean at each layer.

Of the compute bill, 77% goes to the third-order bank (43% for pushing the columns through the weights, 34% for reading pair values out of them), 11% to the covariance and the third-order remainder, 5% to the final-layer skew and the pair formulas, and 3% to the learned parts (counted on a slightly earlier version of the estimator).

03 · error by layer

The error grows by about 1.2e-9 per layer

The error is zero at the first layer and grows by about 1.2e-9 per layer, to 17.2e-9 at the last layer. This is the estimator that is on the board, run on 40 validation networks that were not used for training, measured against the organizers' reference values, which they computed from 1e9 sample inputs per network. The step from one layer to the next grows from 0.5e-9 at layer 3 to 1.8e-9 at layer 15. The 40 networks end between 13.2e-9 and 22.3e-9; their mean, 1.72e-8, matches the 1.712e-8 of the 50 public board networks.

At layer 0 the estimator is exact, because the input is Gaussian; the 0.68e-9 measured there is the sampling noise of the reference values. That noise falls to 0.07e-9 at layer 15 because the unit outputs get smaller with depth, so from layer 5 on more than 90% of the measured error is real.

Each layer's error has two parts. About 83% of the previous layer's error is passed on (80% to 86% at layers 7 to 15), and new error is added: 2.0e-9 at layer 7, rising to 3.8e-9 at layer 15. The learned correction then removes 3% to 5% of the total at each of layers 6 to 15. At layer 15 on the research copy: 11.5e-9 carried in, 3.8e-9 new, 15.8e-9 before the learned correction, 15.0e-9 after it (the board version on the same four networks: 15.2e-9). Because 83% is passed on at each step, an error made at layer 10 still counts about 39% at layer 15, and one made at layer 5 about 15%.

These per-layer parts were measured on the research copy of the estimator, the version used for training, on networks 35, 100, 37 and 39, because the board version does not expose its per-unit inputs. The research copy leaves out a few cost-saving approximations of the board version: its final-layer error is 0.7% to 3.4% lower than the board version's on networks 39, 37 and 35 and 0.6% higher on network 100, with the same per-unit error pattern (correlation 0.99). Every error number that does not need those inputs is from the board version. Networks 35 and 100 were used for training the learned parts; 37 and 39 were not, and the four give the same results.
04 · the three inputs

Three wrong inputs produce the new error

The new error at each layer comes from three per-unit inputs of one formula, the variance, the skew and the kurtosis of each unit's input; the formula itself is accurate. The mean output of a ReLU unit depends on four properties of its input: the mean μ, the standard deviation σ, the skew (lopsidedness) and the excess kurtosis (tail weight). With a = μ/σ, φ the bell curve and Φ its running total, the estimator uses

mean after ReLU = σ·[a·Φ(a) + φ(a)] − σ·skew·a·φ(a)/6 + σ·kurtosis·(a² − 1)·φ(a)/24

Fed with the true four inputs, this formula is wrong by less than 0.1e-9 at every layer from 4 to 15 (networks 35 and 100; the true inputs come from two independent runs of 32 million samples each, so their sampling noise cancels in the comparison). Fed with the estimator's own inputs, each of layers 10 to 15 adds 1.5 to 2.0e-9 through the variance, 0.8 to 1.2e-9 through the skew and 1.0 to 1.3e-9 through the kurtosis (mean of four networks). The three partly cancel, so the combined new error is 2.7 to 3.8e-9, about 15% less than their sum.

Variance. Each unit's variance is off by 0.09% to 0.18% at layers 10 to 15 (root-mean-square over units; the noise of the true values is 0.025%). The variance is read from the carried covariance table, and that table's error builds up with depth: on network 35 its off-diagonal part is off by 0.5% after layer 6, 0.7% after layer 10 and 0.9% after layer 13. About a third of that error at layer 13 (37%) is a single direction close to the mean vector, but that direction causes little of the per-unit variance error: removing the 16 largest error directions would still leave 51% of it, and removing 64 would leave 23%. The error is spread over hundreds of directions.

Skew. The estimator's per-unit skew follows the true skew closely (correlation 0.998 at layer 15 on networks 35 and 100), and what is missing is 4% to 6% of the unit-to-unit spread of the true skew at layers 10 to 15 (1.5% at layer 1). The bank that produces this skew is where 77% of the compute goes.

Kurtosis. The estimator gives every unit of a layer the same kurtosis (0.060 at layer 15). The true values average 0.064 and differ from unit to unit with a spread of 0.016. At layers 8 to 15 the single value is also 5% to 9% below the true average; that offset accounts for 5% to 17% of the kurtosis error and the missing unit-to-unit differences for the rest.

A direct test. On the research copy, replacing the three inputs by their true values at every layer and switching the learned correction off brings the final-layer error from 17.2e-9 to 0.8e-9 (mean of four networks; 0.2e-9 to 1.4e-9 per network). The three inputs therefore account for about 95% of the error. With the learned correction left on, the same replacement gives 4.9e-9 instead of 15.0e-9: the learned correction was trained against the wrong inputs, and with correct inputs it adds error of its own. One input at a time, learned correction on: true variances lower the final error by 34% (25% to 43% on the four networks), true kurtoses by 7% (0% to 14%), true skews by 0% on average (from 11% lower to 4% higher). The single-input results are small or zero because the three errors partly cancel each other and the learned correction is tuned to all three together.

The last layer alone. 05 · the shared part

The collective gain mode

Most of the skew and most of the average kurtosis have one common cause: for a given input the whole previous layer is a little larger or smaller than usual, and that scales the fluctuation of every unit of the next layer up or down together. This shared scaling is called the collective gain mode.

The measurement: run 16 million sample inputs through each of four networks, sort the inputs into 16 groups by the length of the previous layer's output vector, and compute each unit's skew and kurtosis inside the groups. At layer 15 the average excess kurtosis is 0.064 over all inputs and 0.000 inside the groups (between −0.002 and +0.003 on the four networks). The unit-to-unit spread of the skew is 0.106 over all inputs and 0.036 inside the groups, so grouping removes 87% to 90% of the skew variance.

The zero for the kurtosis overstates the effect. Holding the length of a vector fixed lowers the excess kurtosis of any projection of it by itself, by about 6 divided by the width (0.006 here), and that is what the groups show at layer 0, where there is nothing else: -0.0057. So the 0.000 at layer 15 is a remaining positive kurtosis cancelled by this constraint. A cleaner removal of the shared scaling, dividing each sample by its projection on the mean vector, leaves an average excess kurtosis of 0.012 to 0.019 at layers 6 to 15 (network 35, 16 million samples), against 0.033 to 0.061 without it. The shared scaling therefore accounts for about two thirds to four fifths of the average kurtosis, not all of it.

What grouping does not remove is specific to each network. The unit-to-unit spread of the kurtosis is 0.016 over all inputs and still 0.014 inside the groups, and a skew spread of 0.036 remains.

The estimator's errors are in that network-specific part. Its skew error cannot be explained as a wrong size of the shared part or of the rest: a fit of the error on both explains at most 8% of it with equal weight on every unit and at most 17% with the weights that matter for the mean (four networks, layers 1 to 15). The estimator carries a separate vector for the shared part; it holds 35% to 48% of that part at layers 10 to 15, and the column bank holds the remainder.

06 · the limit of per-unit carriers

One number per unit cannot carry what is missing

Even the true kurtosis of every unit at one layer predicts at most 20% of the unit-to-unit differences in kurtosis at the next layer, and for skew less than 2%. So storing one more number per unit cannot carry what is missing: the information is in fourth-order relations between pairs of units, and carrying those is estimated to add 50% to 80% to the compute bill (an earlier team estimate, not re-measured today).

The test used networks 35 and 100 with true values from 32 million samples. The true per-unit values of one layer were pushed through the next layer's weights in four ways (by each upstream unit's share of the variance, with and without the ReLU's response, by squared-weight shares and by absolute shares), and the true values of the next layer were fitted with all of them together. The fit explains 5% to 20% (R² 0.05 to 0.20) across layers 2 to 15. A second independent 32-million-sample estimate of the target correlates 0.87 to 0.996 with the first, so sampling noise is not what limits the fit.

Training. The learned correction now removes 13% of the final-layer error (research copy, four networks: 17.2e-9 without it, 15.0e-9 with it). Our earlier trainings ran a few hundred optimiser steps each. One gradient on one network takes 26 seconds on one processor core, so thousands of steps per day are possible, on 640 training networks. marius_binner's 17.7% lower error in 72 hours at a nearly unchanged budget share, in 24 small steps, is what long training of learned parameters would look like; we do not know its method.

Bill. Our budget share is 12.1%. At the 10% minimum, with today's error, the score would be 1.712e-9, which would be 3rd place today. The current target is a bill cut worth 3.5% at unchanged error, which reaches 1.995e-9: below 2.0e-9, and still 5th place.

What 1e-9 needs. At the 10% minimum it needs a final-layer error of 1.0e-8: 42% below ours and 12% below the best error on the board. The measurements above say how much that is. True variances alone, with no retraining, would give −34%; true variances and kurtoses together −50%. So 1e-9 requires removing most of two of the three input errors, or all three by about half. We have no affordable way to do that. The variance error is spread over hundreds of directions of the covariance table. The skew error is 4% to 6% of a quantity that already takes 77% of the bill. The per-unit kurtosis needs carriers for pairs of units. Sampling the network does not help at this accuracy: the 10% minimum budget pays for about 6,600 full forward passes, which estimate a unit mean with a mean-squared error of about 1e-5, about 600 times our 1.71e-8.

What is left to try is therefore training (how far it goes is not known until it has run) and the bill. If training gave what marius_binner got in three days and the bill reached the minimum, the score would be about 1.409e-9: 1st place today, and still 41% above 1e-9.
